# Amy - First night back

*POV: Amy | Timeline: present day*

---

The hospital discharge papers are in her lap. She hasn't looked at them.

She's looking at the back of Leo's head instead.

He hasn't said much since they brought the wheelchair to her room. Smiled when she looked at him — that particular careful smile she's learning to read, the one that means *I'm fine* and means nothing of the sort. Nick had made a joke she didn't catch the end of, something about hospital food, and Leo had laughed in that way where the laugh doesn't quite reach his eyes before it's already gone.

Now they're on the highway and no one is talking and she can see, from the back seat, the exact moment Leo thinks no one is watching him.

He wipes his eyes with the back of his hand. Quick. Practiced.

Like he's done it before. Like he's been doing it for weeks.

---

Nick is in the passenger seat. He's turned slightly toward the window, and she can only see his profile — the line of his jaw, something held very carefully still in his shoulders. He had hugged her in the hospital corridor like she might disappear if he loosened his grip. She hadn't known what to do with her arms. She'd hugged back because it seemed like the right thing, because he was clearly someone who loved her, because the quality of his relief when she'd squeezed him back had been so raw she'd felt the edge of it even through the fog of the last two weeks.

She still doesn't know him. Not really. Not the way the photographs suggest she should.

And there is something terrible about that — about looking at someone's grief and not being able to reach it from where she's standing.

---

*This is my fault*, she thinks.

Not the accident. She can't own that yet, doesn't have enough of herself back to carry it. But this — the careful smiles, the turned-away faces, the way both of them have been so relentlessly *okay* in her presence, performing a version of okay that must cost them an enormous amount — this she understands. She is the reason for it. She is sitting in this car and she is the source of the wound and she cannot fix it because she cannot even fully see it, cannot reach into the six years she's lost and pull back anything useful, cannot hand Leo the version of herself that would know what to do right now.

The highway hums. Someone changes lanes.

Leo's shoulders rise slightly, then fall.

The panic starts low, the way it always does — a tightening she's come to recognise over the last two weeks as her body deciding something before she does. *Too much. Too much. Nowhere to put it.* She doesn't know who these people are well enough to cry in front of them. She doesn't know who she is well enough to know whether she used to.

---

Then Abhi shifts beside her.

He doesn't say anything first. Just moves his arm — unhurried, like it's the most natural thing — across her shoulders, and pulls her gently into his side.

She goes.

"*I've got you*," he says. Quietly. Just for her.

And the panic, which had been climbing, simply — stops.

---

She doesn't know how to explain it except that it's old, this feeling. Older than anything else she can currently reach. Her body knows it before her mind does: the specific weight of being held by someone who has always held her this way, the same quality of stillness, the same certainty underneath it that nothing further is required of her right now.

She remembers Delhi.

She doesn't remember the six years she's lost, can't find Leo's face in any of the years she's reaching for, can't find the wedding or the house or whatever life they apparently built together. But she remembers Delhi. The terrible street food. The way the ceiling of the hospital room had swum when she opened her eyes. The exact particular misery of being sick and alone in an unfamiliar city at twenty-four.

She had sent him one text. *Not doing great.*

He'd been on the next flight.

She'd woken up to him asleep in the visitor's chair with his head in his hands — that specific pose, she hadn't known then that she'd see it a hundred more times — and she'd said *you didn't have to* and he'd looked up and said *I know* and that had been the end of it.

---

She rests her head against his shoulder now.

Outside, Mumbai is doing what Mumbai does — perpetually, indifferently, enormously itself. Buildings and hoardings and the grey-green of the bay visible briefly between structures before the road curves away. She watches it. She breathes.

In the front, Leo wipes his eyes again. Nick's jaw stays tight.

She can't fix it. She knows that. Whatever she is missing, whatever shape of herself will eventually surface to meet them where they are — she can't manufacture it right now, can't apologise her way into the six years she owes them back.

But she is here. She is being brought home. Abhi's arm is around her and she is not panicking and that is, for right now, the precise limit of what she is able to manage.

*I've got you.*

It occurs to her — slowly, the way everything occurs to her lately — that this has always been true. That some part of her has known it for longer than she can currently remember.

The car moves through the city.

She stays where she is.

 The room was dark when she woke.

Not the thin, monitored dark of the hospital — no strip of light under the door, no soft beep of something measuring her. This was fuller. Warmer. She lay still for a moment, listening to it.

The armchair across from her held Abhi's shape. Knees folded at an angle that would be painful by morning, head dropped sideways, breathing slow. He'd refused the bed with such quiet finality that she hadn't argued. *I sleep in chairs fine,* he'd said, which was obviously untrue, and she'd let it go because his face had made it clear the subject was closed.

She sat up carefully.

Her throat was dry — properly dry, the kind that had probably woken her — and she looked around the room for water and found none and remembered she didn't know where anything was. She didn't know this house. She'd been told it was hers.

She got up.

The door opened without sound. The hallway was dark except for a thin wash of light from somewhere further on, and she was so focused on the direction of it — kitchen, probably, kitchen meant water — that she almost didn't see him.

Leo.

He was sitting on the floor outside her door. Back against the wall, knees bent, one arm resting across them, head dropped forward into the crook of his elbow. Asleep. Not pretending to sleep, not stationed there in any official capacity — just asleep, the way someone falls asleep when they've been sitting very still for a very long time and lost the fight.

She stood in the doorway and looked at him.

He woke the moment she moved. Not slowly — all at once, head coming up, eyes finding her in the dark with an immediacy that suggested some part of him had been listening even through sleep. He didn't scramble. Didn't make it strange. Just looked at her the way she was starting to recognise: like he was doing a count of something.

"Thirsty," she said, which was explanation enough.

He got up in one motion and she followed him down the hall to the kitchen without being asked, and he found a glass without turning on any lights because he knew where everything was, and he filled it and handed it to her and stood back while she drank.

"You were on the floor," she said.

"I was."

"Outside my door."

He leaned against the counter. "I didn't want to be far." A pause. "In case."

She thought about asking *in case of what* and decided it wasn't the real question. He'd slept on the floor outside her room because he'd needed to be close to something he couldn't reach and the floor was the closest he could get without — she didn't know the end of that thought. She was still learning the dimensions of what she'd missed.

"Did you sleep at all?"

"A bit." He looked unbothered by this. "Are you hungry?"

"It's the middle of the night."

"I have mango ice cream."

She opened her mouth to say *no, it's fine, I'm fine,* which was what she'd been saying to everyone for three days, and then she didn't. Something in his delivery — patient, no pressure, just the fact of it sitting there — made the refusal feel like the wrong answer.

"Yes," she said. "Okay."

He found bowls. He found spoons. He carried both to the couch with the ease of someone who'd done it many times before and she followed him and sat, and for a moment they both ate in silence.

The room was — she looked around.

Bright, even in low light. Colours that shouldn't work together and somehow did: the throw on the arm of the couch, deep orange, against cushions that were teal and patterned with something she couldn't quite make out. Framed prints on the walls that she'd need daylight to read properly. A shelf near the window crowded with small things — she couldn't tell what they were from here but there were many of them, arranged without symmetry, obviously by someone who knew exactly where everything went. Flowers, dried now but still intact, in a glass bottle on the side table.

It looked, she thought, like someone had made it. Not decorated — *made* it. Like every object had been chosen because it said something.

"I did all of this?"

Leo followed her eyes around the room. "Every single thing." He said it simply. "You argued with me about the cushions for about three weeks. I thought I'd lost, and then I came home one day and they were just there."

She looked at the cushions. Something about them — the particular shade, the pattern — felt almost familiar in the way a word on the tip of the tongue felt familiar. Present but not graspable.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm sorry I can't—"

"Don't." Not sharp. Just certain. "You don't need to apologise to me."

"It seems like something you should be apologised to for."

He was quiet for a moment. Turned his spoon over.

"It hurts," he said. "I won't tell you it doesn't. I look at you and I can see that you're—" He stopped. Started again. "But you're here. And you're safe. And I would wait much longer than this if I had to." He glanced at her sideways, and there was something in it that wasn't quite a smile but was close. "We were worth it. We *are* worth it. Both things."

She didn't know what to say to that.

She wasn't sure she'd earned it yet — that certainty of his, the easy way he said *we* like it was still accurate, still a current thing she had access to. But she also thought she understood, dimly, what Abhi had meant at the hospital when he'd said *your husband loves you very much.* He'd said it as a fact, not a comfort. The distinction had stayed with her.

She looked at the flowers in the bottle. At the small crowded shelf. At the cushions she had apparently argued for and won.

Somewhere in this house was six years she couldn't find. But whoever had assembled this room had known what she loved — had built a space that looked, without her being able to explain why, like somewhere she would have chosen. Like herself.

Leo refilled her bowl without asking and she let him.

Outside, the city was quiet. The couch was soft. The ice cream was cold and sweet and exactly right, and she ate it in the dark beside a man she didn't remember and felt, for the first time since she'd woken up in that white room, like she was somewhere she might eventually recognise.
