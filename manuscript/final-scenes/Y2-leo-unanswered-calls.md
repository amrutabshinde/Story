The candles on the table were flickering against the dimmed lights of the restaurant, and Natasha was in the middle of telling me about her work presentation when my phone vibrated against the tablecloth.

I glanced down. *Amy.*

My first thought wasn't concern—it was lighthearted annoyance. Amy was always around, always calling about some mundane thing, or usually asking if we had leftover takeout in the fridge. And right then, I was trying to be a good boyfriend to Nat, who had already complained twice that week about me being distracted.

"Sorry, let me just..." I tapped the green button and put the phone to my ear. Before Amy could even utter a syllable, I cut straight in. "Amy, hey—listen, I’m actually in the middle of dinner with Nat right now. Can this wait? I’ll call you later tonight, okay?"

I didn't give her a second to reply. I ended the call, set the phone face down on the table, and gave Natasha an apologetic smile. "Sorry about that. Where were we?"

Twenty minutes later, my phone started vibrating again. Then again. And again.

By the fourth consecutive call from Abhi, Natasha gave me an annoyed look. "Are you going to get that?"

"It’s Abhi. He knows I'm out," I muttered, picking up. "Hey, man, what's so urgent—"

"Where the *hell* are you, Leo?" Abhi’s voice didn't just cut through the speaker; it practically vibrated with a dangerous, quiet rage I had rarely heard from him.

"I’m at dinner with Nat, why—"

"Amy is stranded on the highway outside Mumbai," Abhi interrupted, his tone cold enough to freeze water. "Her state transport bus blew an engine and caught fire on the shoulder. Ash and I are stuck in Pune in traffic."

The air was instantly sucked out of my lungs. The restaurant noise faded into a dull buzz.

"What?" my voice cracked.

"She’s been standing on the dusty highway in forty-degree heat for over an hour trying to hail a ride home," Abhi growled. "She didn't want to tell me, but I called to check up on her because I knew her bus was running late. If you don't get your ass out there right now, Leo, I swear to God—"

"I’m going. I’m going right now," I stammered, already standing up so fast my chair scraped loudly against the floor.

I barely remembered blurted apologies to Natasha, throwing cash onto the table, or sprinting to my car. All I could process was the sickening weight of my own stupidity pressing down on my chest. I hadn't even let her speak. She had been standing helpless on a burning asphalt road, and I had dismissed her like an inconvenience.

The drive down the highway was twenty minutes of pure, agonizing panic. I gripped the steering wheel so hard my knuckles turned white, scanning the dusty roadside ahead.

And then I saw her.

A mile past the toll booth, her tiny frame looked swallowed up by the vast expanse of grey concrete and dry dirt. The broken-down bus was parked further down, but Amy had walked away from the crowd, standing by the gravel shoulder with her bag slung over her shoulder, shielding her eyes from the blinding glare of the afternoon sun. She was pale, sweat bead on her forehead, her hair sticking to her neck.

I slammed on the brakes, pulling the car onto the shoulder right in front of her, sending up a cloud of dust.

I scrambled out of the driver's side and rushed toward her. "Amy!"

She didn't rush toward me. She didn't look relieved. She simply stopped walking, turned her head slowly, and looked at me through her glasses. Her face was completely devoid of color, but her eyes were cold, sharp, and blazing with quiet fury.

"Amy, I'm so sorry," I breathed, reaching out instinctively toward her arm, but she took a deliberate step back, out of my reach.

"I don't need a ride, Leo," she said. Her voice was flat, tight, and clipped. "I'm hailing a cab."

"Amy, there are no cabs coming out this far on the highway," I said. "Please. Get in the car. It’s brutal out here."

"I am already waiting for past one hour, another hour won't kill me. I'd rather wait," she said, looking straight past me at the oncoming traffic.

That hit me harder than a physical punch. I didn't try to defend myself. I didn't say the phone connection was bad, I didn't say I thought it was a casual call, and I didn't bring up Nat. There was no excuse in the world that made what I did okay.

"You're right," I said quietly, stepping back slightly to give her space, but staying firmly between her and the highway traffic. "I was an idiot. A complete, selfish, reckless idiot. I didn't let you speak, I assumed, and I left you stranded out here in this heat."

Amy kept her eyes fixed on the road, her jaw set tight, refusing to look at me.

"I'm not going to ask you to forgive me right now," I continued, my voice raw with absolute sincerity. "You have every right to be furious with me. Honestly, if you want to slap me right across the face right now, go ahead. Do it. I won't even flinch. I deserve it."

Her eye twitched slightly, but she stayed silent, her arms crossed tightly over her chest.

"I am going to feel guilty about this for the rest of my life," I said, looking right at her, heart on my sleeve. "Every time you pick up the phone, every time you need a ride, every single day—I'm going to remember leaving you here, and I'm going to hate myself for it. But please, Amy... don't punish yourself just to punish me. It's almost forty degrees. Just get in the car. You can yell at me the entire ride back to Mumbai. You can ignore me for a month. Just let me take you home."

I walked back to the car, opened the passenger door wide, and reached inside to crank the air conditioning up to its maximum setting. Cold air began blasting out of the vents into the sweltering heat. Then I grabbed a fresh, sealed bottle of cold water from the center console, walked back to her, and held it out.

I didn't push her. I didn't badger her anymore. I just stood there next to her in the stifling, dusty heat, holding the water, willing to stand out there until she was ready.

Amy stared at the bottle in my hand for a long, quiet ten seconds. The silence between us was heavy, filled only with the distant hum of highway traffic.

Slowly, her shoulders relaxed just a fraction of an inch. She let out a long, quiet exhale, her sharp glare softening into her usual dry, exasperated mask.
She snatched the water bottle from my hand without making eye contact and walking past me toward the open passenger door.

A massive, pathetic wave of relief washed over me. 

I carefully closed the door after she got in, rushed around to the driver's side, and slid into the cool interior. As I merged back onto the highway, I kept casting worried, tentative glances at her every few seconds. She had unbuckled her bag, taken a slow sip of the cold water, and leaned her head back against the headrest, staring out the window.

She was still angry with me—I knew that. I knew I had a long way to go before I made this up to her. But as I gripped the steering wheel with nervous intensity, determined to drive her home safely, I knew one thing for certain: I was never going to dismiss her again.
