I was managing the event for my college reunion that night. The job wasn’t exactly glamorous, but the money was solid, and the band I usually tour-managed was on a three-month hiatus. Everything was running like clockwork, so around mid-evening, I decided to step behind the bar to help the staff handle the crowd.

I spotted plenty of familiar faces from my college days a few years back. Then I saw Amy walk through the entrance.

I didn’t know her personally back in school, but she was hard to miss—one of the few who’d bagged a massive package during campus placements, turning her into a bit of a campus celebrity overnight. Tonight, though, she looked noticeably out of place. While everyone else was loudly mingling and catching up, she slipped quietly past the crowd and took a seat on a bar stool right in front of me.

"One virgin mojito, please," she said.

"Sure thing," I replied, grabbing a glass and the muddle.

She looked at me for a moment, her eyes lighting up with sudden recognition. "Hey, I know you! You designed the lighting for that dance performance that won us the inter-college competition. I don't think our college had ever even been shortlisted before that."

"Thanks!" I said, feeling a genuine flash of pride. I’d handled the stage lighting and audio for that performance—the exact night I realized this line of work was what I actually wanted to do. "Well, everyone knows you, of course. The legend who secured the biggest paycheck."

"Completely unexpectedly," she added with a self-deprecating smile.

"I have to admit, I didn't think this kind of reunion was your scene," I said, remembering how she used to hang around the eco-club crowd who organized weekend treks and tree-planting drives.

"It isn't, honestly," she admitted, leaning against the counter. "But I needed an excuse to escape a disastrous dinner. This was the best option on short notice."

"That bad, huh?" I chuckled.

"You have no idea."

She ended up staying right there at the bar for the rest of the evening, even while the main performances were happening on stage. We talked about what she was doing now and what I was planning next. She told me about her sprawling, chaotic family living in Pune, and listened with genuine fascination as I described growing up in Dalhousie. We talked about travel, adventure sports, and the random places we’d both visited.

It was refreshing—a completely low-stakes conversation. No strings attached, no flirtatious subtext. Given how different our worlds were, neither of us was trying to hook up, and that took all the pressure off. I didn't have to try to impress her, and she didn't have to put on a show.

My assistant, Tej, clearly interpreted things differently. He made sure no one bothered me with venue issues, blown fuses, or running low on liquor. He aggressively fended off the event staff because he genuinely believed I was finally giving a girl a chance. The guy was a hopeless romantic.

By the time the venue started clearing out, Amy checked her wrist. "It's getting really late. I should probably head out."

"I can call you a cab, or drop you off if you want," I offered.

"I live pretty close by. I'll just walk," she said, carefully sliding off the bar stool. "This was fun."

"Unexpectedly," I added, echoing her earlier words with a smile.

She laughed. Just then, Tej called out from across the floor, "We'll wrap up here, boss! You go ahead—don't leave the lady to walk back home alone so late." He flashed a unsubtle wink at me.

Amy rolled her eyes playfully as we stepped outside. "Friends trying to set you up?"

"Unfortunately."

"I swear..." she muttered. "People are obsessed with relationships."

It hit me then—the drink, combined with whatever had happened at her dinner earlier, was definitely catching up to her. She looked like an angry, flustered panda as she stormed toward the main gate.

We started walking.

"They really are. They act like it's the only thing that matters." There was a sharpness in her voice now. "If you're single after a certain age, everyone starts looking at you like something must be wrong."

I glanced sideways at her.She wasn't really talking to me anymore.

"They ask if you're 'seeing someone.'If you say no, they ask why. If you say you're happy, they don't believe you. If you say you're not looking, they tell you you'll change your mind." She let out a bitter laugh."It's exhausting."

I stayed quiet.

"Relationships are so overrated anyway," she blurted out, a sudden edge of heat in her voice. "You think you’ve found someone great, and then he just goes off with the first shiny distraction that comes along."

A few minutes later I picked up a sealed bottle of mango liqueur from the event bar we'd packed up and poured us each a tiny glass.

"Try this."

"What is it?"

"Mango."

She took a cautious sip.Her eyes widened.

"Oh."

Another sip.

"Oh, that's dangerous."

"I know."

As we walked down the quiet street, the alcohol fully took hold. Soon she was swaying slightly, needing to loop her arm through mine for balance while I carried her sandals in my other hand.

"It's like... the world constantly tells you that you have to find someone," she slurred, gesturing loosely with her empty glass. "And if you don't, there must be something wrong with you. They repeat it so many times you almost start believing the bullshit."

"Yeah. Screw that," I agreed softly.

"Exactly," she muttered.

"Honestly, I don't even have time for a relationship right now," I said as we turned down her lane. "I'm working eighteen-hour days, and I'm totally fine with it. I don't want the weight of anyone's expectations on me."

"Right?" she agreed emphatically. "We should just be able to meet people, have a good time, and say goodbye. Simple."

"Agreed."

We reached her apartment building, and I walked her right up to her door. She fumbled with her keys for a moment before unlocking it, pushing the door open, and tossing her shoes unceremoniously onto the entryway floor.

She turned back to me, her head tilting slightly as she leaned against the doorframe, her eyelids heavy. "Do you want to come in?"

My heart hammered against my ribs. Every natural instinct in me wanted to say yes. Looking at her standing there in the soft doorway light, I wanted nothing more than to step inside, close the door, and stay.

"Amy... I think I should go," I said softly, forcing my voice to stay steady.

"Oh, c'mon..." she slurred, reaching out loosely toward my sleeve.

"Hey," I said gently, catching her wrist and holding her steady. "You're drunk, and you're not thinking straight tonight."

She blinked at me, her expression a mix of confusion and sleepiness.

I reached into my pocket, pulled out a pen, and grabbed a piece of paper from my wallet. I scribbled my number on it and tucked it securely into her palm, folding her fingers over it. 

"Here," I said quietly, looking into her eyes. "This is my number. If you wake up tomorrow morning, feeling clear-headed, and you still want to catch up... call me."

She looked down at her hand, nodding vaguely.

"Get some sleep, Amy," I said.

I waited until she stepped inside.Until I heard the deadbolt slide into place.Only then did I let myself breathe.She'd probably wake up tomorrow, cringe at herself, throw away the piece of paper, and forget we'd ever met.That was probably for the best.I turned and walked back into the quiet night.
