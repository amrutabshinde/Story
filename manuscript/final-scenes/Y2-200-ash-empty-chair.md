I’ve known for months that Amy has a crush on Leo. It’s not particularly hard to spot if you’re paying attention—the way her eyes track him when he enters a room, the way she automatically remembers how he takes his coffee, the quiet, stubborn way she looks after him without ever asking for a thank-you.

What Leo hasn't realized yet—because Leo is a blind idiot when it comes to his own heart—is that he likes her back. He calls it "looking out for Abhi's friend" or "having a good rhythm," but you don't spend six months relying on a person for your sanity without letting them take root inside you.

The proof was sitting right at our table at his office award ceremony.

It was a formal setup: Abhi had Tanu beside him, I had Rach, and Leo had Natasha sitting on his right, looking polished and perfectly suited for an corporate gala. But right across from Leo was an empty velvet chair that stayed empty all evening.

Every three minutes, Leo’s eyes drifted. To the double glass doors of the banquet hall. To the empty chair. To his phone sitting face-up on the tablecloth, entirely silent. Every time the heavy doors swung open for late arrivals, his shoulders would tense slightly, only to sink back down when it wasn't her.

When his name was called for the major project award, he went up, gave a brilliant, sharp speech, and remembered to thank his team, Abhi, and me. He smiled for the company photographer, took the trophy, and spent the rest of the evening doing the rounds—dancing with Nat, laughing with the management, drinking scotch with us.

To anyone else, he looked on top of the world. But I could see the visible dent Amy’s absence had carved into him. He was performing happiness, but his eyes kept returning to that empty place setting. I sat there wondering if Leo even understood why the room felt so quiet to him, or if he was still telling himself she was just another friend who flaked.

The next afternoon, Amy walked into our flat like nothing had happened.

Abhi was out running errands. Leo and I were on the couch in the living room, a cricket match playing on the TV.

Amy let herself in, tossed her keys on the side table, and headed toward the kitchen. "Hey, guys," she said lightly. "Is there any leftov—"

"Where were you last night?" Leo’s voice cut through the room, sharp and abrupt.

Amy paused by the kitchen counter, her hand hovering over a mug. She kept her face carefully smooth—a dead giveaway that she was bracing herself. "I was in office. We had a deadline."

Leo stood up from the couch. "Why didn't you come to the awards? We waited for you."

Amy tried to brush it off, giving a small, breezy wave of her hand as if it were a silly question. "I didn't know I was expected."

"Are you serious right now?" Leo’s voice rose, the suppressed frustration from the night before spilling out all at once. "I asked you to come. I literally texted you the address twice."

Amy swallowed, stepping back slightly against the counter. "But Abhi, Tanu, Ash, Rach, and Nat were there," she said, her voice dropping an octave. "I didn't think it mattered if I came."

"It mattered to me," Leo said cleanly.

The words hung heavy in the living room. Amy flinched, looking away toward the balcony. "Leo, c'mon... I just didn't want to be a bother," she mumbled. "You had the guys and your girlfriends. I didn't want to be a third wheel all evening..."

Leo looked as if she had physically slapped him across the face.

Even sitting on the couch, I felt the sting of it. A *bother*? A *third wheel*? We had never, not once, thought of Amy as an extra or a bother. She was built into the very foundation of how our apartment functioned.

But as I looked at her small, rigid posture, I knew why she had stayed away. It hadn't been about work or deadlines. It was the quiet, unbearable torture of having to sit at a table for four hours watching Leo wrap his arm around Natasha.

"Is that what you think?" Leo asked, his voice low, wounded, and angry.

"Leo..." she tried softly.

"No, I’m not done," he snapped, cutting her off completely.

He turned on his heel, walked down the hallway to his room, and came back ten seconds later holding a small, dark velvet box. He didn't hand it to her gently; he practically forced it into her hands.

"I got this for you," Leo said bitterly. "I wanted to give it to you after the award... which you didn't care enough to show up for."

Amy stared down at the box. Her fingers trembled as she popped the latch open. Inside sat a delicate silver bracelet.

My chest tighted. I had been with Leo the afternoon he bought it. He’d dragged me through three different jewelers, until he found this exact one. He got it as a thank-you for the grueling six months when his project was burning him alive—those endless nights when Amy would stay up till 2 a.m. making him tea, leaving hot food on his desk, dicussing solutions and keeping him grounded when he was ready to snap.

Inside the box, tucked beneath the velvet lining, was the small card he had made me write down with him: *For keeping me fed and sane for the last 6 months.*

Amy looked up from the bracelet, her eyes wide, glistening, and completely filled with shock. "Leo... I am so sorry."

"It's fine," Leo said.

It was definitively, glaringly not fine. Without another word, he walked back down the corridor, stepped into his room, and shut the door slowly, the click of the lock echoing through the silent flat.

Amy stood frozen in the middle of the living room, staring at the closed wooden door as if waiting for it to reopen. When it didn't, she carefully snapped the velvet box shut, clutched it against her ribs, turned around, and walked straight out the front door.

I didn't pause to think. I kicked off my house slides, grabbed my shoes, and bolted after her.

By the time I hit the hallway, the elevator display was already lighting up, tracking its movement down to the ground floor. "Dammit," I muttered under my breath.

I turned toward the fire exit, shoved the heavy door open, and ran down four flights of concrete stairs two steps at a time, my knees aching with every slam of my feet. I burst through the lobby doors just in time to see her walking fast toward the building’s main gate, her head down, her shoulders hunched inward.

"Amy!" I called out, jogging up behind her.

She froze near the security post and turned around. Her face was flushed, and her cheeks were wet with tears that she was frantically trying to rub away with the back of her sleeve. "Ash... go back upstairs," she choked out. "I'm fine. Really, I just—"

I knew how much she carried under that relentless, cheerful front—how much it cost her to constantly pretend she was just 'one of the guys' while watching the person she loved belong to someone else.

I didn't say a word. I reached out, took her hand to stop her from wiping her eyes, and pulled her straight into a tight, firm hug.

For a second, she went rigid in my arms, holding her breath as if trying to keep the dam from breaking. Then, with a shaky, shuddering exhale,  her head collapsed against my shoulder, and she began to sob—quiet, heavy, heartbreaking sobs that she had been holding in all this time.

I just held on tighter, keeping my hand pressed against the back of her head, standing between her and the street so nobody else could see her fall apart.
